# Claim 3: BYOD RPM nationally applicable for U.S. rural populations

## Bottom line
BYOD RPM is nationally feasible and increasingly practical, but it is not uniformly universal across rural America. The policy and vendor stack is real: Medicare reimburses RPM/RTM, HHS recognizes asynchronous/store-and-forward telehealth, and vendors already ship cellular or app-based RPM workflows nationally. The limiting factors are access, not concept: smartphone ownership is high but not universal, seniors lag, rural broadband is still weaker than suburban access, and some rural patients will need cellular gateways or store-and-forward workflows rather than app-only designs.

## (a) Smartphone / smartwatch ownership, including rural and seniors
**Verdict: PARTIALLY SUPPORTED**

**Rationale:** Pew shows the device base is large enough to make BYOD RPM workable nationally: 91% of U.S. adults own smartphones, and 37% own smartwatches. But adoption drops for older adults (78% smartphone ownership and 24% smartwatch ownership for age 65+), and Pew continues to note that rural adults are less likely than urban adults to own smartphones and less likely than suburban adults to have home broadband. So the demographic base is strong, but BYOD cannot assume every rural or older patient already has the needed device stack.

**3 strongest sources:**
1. Pew mobile fact sheet — 91% smartphone ownership; 78% among adults 65+
2. Pew 2024 mobile technology/home broadband report — rural broadband and smartphone-dependent gaps
3. Pew smartwatch charts — 37% overall; 24% for age 65+

## (b) National RPM reimbursement landscape
**Verdict: SUPPORTED**

**Rationale:** CMS clearly covers remote patient monitoring in Medicare and breaks it into setup, device supply, and treatment-management components. CMS also states the same payment logic applies regardless of the specific device type, and the agency’s MLN guidance ties RPM/RTM to secure electronic transmission, consent, and day-count rules. RTM is also a standing Medicare code family, so nationally there is a reimbursement pathway for both physiologic RPM and therapeutic monitoring.

**3 strongest sources:**
1. CMS Remote Patient Monitoring page — Medicare broadly covers RPM and defines the core components
2. CMS MLN901705 booklet — patient consent, secure upload, and billing rules
3. CMS manual/system documents — RTM codes 98975/98976/98977/98980/98981

## (c) Existing BYOD-compatible RPM platforms and home diagnostic kits nationally
**Verdict: SUPPORTED**

**Rationale:** There are already national vendors built for low-friction monitoring. Tenovi is the clearest example: its cellular gateway works with 60+ FDA-cleared devices, needs no app or Wi-Fi, and pushes data over cellular networks. Optimize Health and Healthie also show the broader national platform layer: software plus patient-friendly devices/support, and support for wearable/mobile-app/home-device workflows. That means BYOD-compatible RPM is not hypothetical; it is already a commercial category.

**3 strongest sources:**
1. Tenovi Gateway page — 60+ devices, no app, no Wi-Fi, cellular transmission
2. Optimize Health remote healthcare page — RPM software with patient-friendly devices and support
3. Healthie remote monitoring glossary — wearable devices, mobile apps, and home-based monitoring devices

## (d) Broadband constraints in rural U.S. and store-and-forward as a solution
**Verdict: PARTIALLY SUPPORTED**

**Rationale:** Rural connectivity is a real constraint. Pew reports rural adults trail suburban adults on home broadband, and FCC’s 2024 broadband reporting shows the federal benchmark gap is still meaningful in rural areas. Store-and-forward is a recognized telehealth modality: HHS explicitly defines asynchronous telehealth as store-and-forward and lists text, images, and report uploads as valid workflows. However, it is only a partial solution for RPM because Medicare’s normal telehealth rules are still largely synchronous, and CMS’s explicit store-and-forward billing example is narrow (G2010, largely tied to specific circumstances rather than a universal RPM workaround).

**3 strongest sources:**
1. FCC Internet Access Services: Status as of June 30, 2024 — national broadband baseline
2. HHS asynchronous telehealth guide — store-and-forward recognized as a telehealth modality
3. CMS telemedicine fact sheet — G2010 store-and-forward is recognized but limited

## (e) The national gap
**Verdict: SUPPORTED**

**Rationale:** The gap is not whether RPM exists nationally; it does. The gap is that universal national applicability requires design choices to bridge uneven device ownership, older-adult adoption, and rural broadband constraints. That is exactly where BYOD plus cellular gateways plus store-and-forward can fill the need: it reduces hardware procurement burden and makes deployment possible in lower-bandwidth communities. The remaining gap is operational, not conceptual—patient onboarding, data transport, and reimbursement alignment still need to be configured to local access realities.

**3 strongest sources:**
1. Pew smartphone and broadband reports — high overall adoption but rural/older gaps remain
2. CMS RPM/RTM guidance — reimbursement is available, but only under defined rules
3. Tenovi / Healthie / Optimize Health — the market already supports low-friction and BYOD-friendly workflows

## Conclusion
BYOD RPM is nationally applicable in the sense that there is a real U.S.-wide reimbursement and vendor ecosystem to support it, and the majority of adults already have the basic devices needed. But it is only partially applicable as a blanket assumption for all rural patients, because rural broadband and older-adult device ownership remain uneven. In practice, the strongest national model is not “BYOD only”; it is BYOD where possible, cellular gateway / bundled kit where needed, and store-and-forward for low-bandwidth follow-up and intake.
